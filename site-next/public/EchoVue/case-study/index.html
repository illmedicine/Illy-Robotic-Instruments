Cover story · The future of the smart home

The Home That Finally Knows You're Home.

Smart homes promised to understand us, and mostly learned our voices. Echo Vue listens to something else: the room itself. A $50 plate on the wall, the WiFi you already have, and an AI that knows a hallway is busy without ever seeing a face.

By DeMarkus Wilson · Founder & CEO, Illy Robotic Instruments · Concept renders · October 2026

Left: Illy Bridge 2, mounted beside an ordinary light switch. Right: Illy Bridge Pro. Concept renders of pre-production housings; the screens show the real interface with illustrative values.

A motel is one of the most honest buildings in America. Every door opens onto a stranger's night, and every hallway, lobby and laundry room is a place where those strangers briefly overlap. I spent three weeks inside that overlap at a Motel 6. What I came away with was not a complaint. It was a question: why do buildings know so much about their bookings and so little about how their own spaces are actually used?

The usual answer is a camera. I didn't want a camera. Nobody walking to the ice machine signed up for one. So I started from a different idea: the building was already full of a signal that moves when people do. It was the WiFi.

The smartest room in the building shouldn't need an eye.

IoT meets AI

Google Home was the idea. This is the home.

The first wave of the smart home taught our houses to hear us. Say a word and a light comes on. It was a good trick and it was always a little backward: the home waited to be told.

Echo Vue flips it. The room is the sensor. Walk into a hallway and the system already knows it's occupied, how much is moving, and, when someone is still, the slow rhythm of their breathing. The Internet of Things gave us devices that report. AI gives them the ability to understand. Echo Vue puts both behind one plate on the wall, with no camera and nothing to wear.

If Google Home was the promise, this is the delivery.

The case study

The part nobody put in the brochure.

Building it taught me something I did not expect. The hard problem was not the signal processing. It was that radio-frequency data sits in a legal and ethical gray area: it is invisible, it is passive, it is not a picture, and the rules we have for cameras and recordings do not clearly describe it.

The difference that mattered most is transparency. A camera announces itself. A radio-frequency sensor does not, and the person being sensed has no way to notice, object or opt out unless the operator builds that in. When something is invisible, the ethical burden moves entirely onto the people who deploy it.

Questions the law does not clearly answer

These are open questions, not conclusions: what I ran into when I tried to find out whether this kind of sensing was clearly allowed, clearly prohibited, or neither.

Is it content, or just physics?

Wiretap and eavesdropping laws were written to protect the content of communications. CSI does not read anyone's messages; it reads how the channel itself is disturbed. Whether that falls inside or outside rules built for communications is not obvious.

Is there an expectation of privacy in a signal?

Courts have wrestled with technology that senses what people cannot, for example thermal imaging of a home in Kyllo v. United States (2001). That case concerned government use, and a private hotel operator is governed differently, which is part of the uncertainty. Radio that passes through walls raises the same kind of question.

Is a body's RF signature a biometric?

Biometric privacy statutes were written around things like fingerprints, face geometry and voiceprints. A pattern of breathing or movement inferred from radio is not obviously on those lists, but it is derived from a person's body. It is unclear whether it should be treated as one.

When does presence become health data?

A breathing rate is a vital sign. Collected about identifiable people, it starts to look like health information. Collected as an anonymous rhythm in a corridor, it may not. The line depends on whether anyone can be tied to the data.

Who consented?

In a public area, guests are free to walk through. Do they consent to being measured by something they cannot detect? Signage helps, but it is easy to overlook, and there is no shared standard for what an RF notice should say.

Radio does not respect room boundaries

A sensor in a hallway can pick up effects from beyond the wall. That is a practical privacy risk with no camera equivalent, and it cannot be settled by policy alone. It has to be handled in placement and in what the system is able to do.

None of this had a clean answer I could point to. So I stopped asking what I could get away with and started asking what a person who did not know they were being sensed would reasonably want me to have done. I decided to design for the strictest plausible interpretation.

Privacy by architecture

Sense, don't watch.

A policy can be broken; an architecture that cannot do something is harder to break. These are the choices made in Echo Vue, and the reason behind each.

No images or audio in the sensing pipeline

Why: Optical and audio data identify people. The goal was occupancy, not identity.

How: The RF sensing pipeline never captures or transmits images or audio. The Illy Bridge 2 has no camera, microphone or speaker at all. The Pro adds a speaker and microphone, and is planned to offer an optional camera and biometric scanner; those are separate modules, which are opt-in, need the explicit consent of the people they affect, and need legal review where deployed, biometrics most of all.

Public areas only

Why: Guest rooms carry the strongest expectation of privacy.

How: Hallways, lobby, pool, laundry and other common areas are the only places sensed. Guest rooms are only counted on the dashboard, never listed or read, and the backend rejects any sensing zone named like a room, suite or bathroom.

Features, not raw signal

Why: Raw channel data holds far more than the question being asked.

How: Each bridge reduces the radio signal to a few motion features per second on the device itself. Raw CSI frames never leave it.

Anonymised nearby-device counts

Why: A Bluetooth address is a persistent identifier for a person's device.

How: Addresses are hashed on the bridge with a random salt that rotates every 24 hours; only 3 bytes of the hash are kept, and the backend rejects anything that is not that shape. The dashboard sees counts and signal strength, never addresses.

Estimates are labelled as estimates

Why: Overclaiming accuracy about people is an ethics problem, not only an engineering one.

How: Breathing figures and any people-or-pets split carry an 'estimate' label. With no usable signal the screen says 'no breathing signal' or 'not counted' instead of guessing. Live presence readings are never simulated, and heart rate is not reported because it cannot be measured reliably this way.

Short memory

Why: The less that is kept, the less can be misused.

How: Live state is held in memory. For the History page the backend also keeps anonymous, aggregated counts per minute (how occupied an area was, how many devices were nearby, breathing rates with no identity attached) for 90 days, and then deletes them. It never stores raw radio data, device addresses, or anything that identifies a person, and there is no database of RF signatures.

One honest caveat: keeping guest rooms out of scope depends on where bridges are placed, with a name check on the backend as a backstop, not a guarantee on its own.

Our commitments

Ethics isn't a feature. It's the floor.

Illy Robotic Instruments builds sensing and AI products. The people who build technology that can observe others owe those people more than legal compliance, particularly while the law is still catching up. We commit to:

  1. Notice and consent first. Sensing should never be invisible to the people it measures. Deployments need clear notice, and consent is a condition of operating, not an afterthought.
  2. Public spaces only. We do not sense private rooms. Where the physics of radio could reach them, we design placement and software to keep them out.
  3. Collect the minimum. Derived features instead of raw signal, anonymised identifiers instead of addresses, and short memory instead of archives.
  4. Presence, never identity. Echo Vue answers whether anyone is there and how busy a space is. It is not built to identify anyone: even the nearby-device hint uses ids that reset daily and says only that a device has been around for a while.
  5. Honesty about what AI can and cannot tell. Estimates are labelled as estimates. When the signal is not good enough, the system says so rather than inventing a number, and live presence readings are never simulated.
  6. Human accountability. Operators decide where sensing happens: a bridge only senses the area it is placed in and assigned to. They remain responsible for how it is used. A model's output is an input to a human decision, not a replacement for it.
  7. Open to scrutiny. We publish how the system works, including its limits, and we welcome legal, ethical and technical review. We expect deployments to have legal review in their own jurisdiction before they go live.

The fact that a signal is invisible is a reason for more care, not less.

Still open

What responsible deployment still requires.

Echo Vue is a prototype, and a case study is not a compliance programme. Before anything like it is used on real guests, these remain to be done:

  • Review by an attorney in the jurisdiction of use, covering wiretap and eavesdropping, consumer-protection, biometric and health-data rules, and landlord and guest rights.
  • Plain-language notice in the places that are sensed, and a way for guests and staff to ask questions or object.
  • A written retention and access policy, including how requests from third parties would be handled.
  • A formal privacy impact assessment and independent testing of the accuracy claims made about breathing and occupancy.
  • A conversation with regulators, researchers and privacy advocates, because the standards for this kind of sensing should not be written by the people who profit from it alone.
