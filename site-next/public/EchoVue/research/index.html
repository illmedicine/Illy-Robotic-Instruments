Research · The evolution of Echo Vue

From Spectacle to Signal.

Seven months, one project, two ideas of what it means to see a room. How Echo Vue went from a glittering 3D skeleton built on a camera to a plain, honest readout on the wall.

By DeMarkus Wilson · Founder & CEO, Illy Robotic Instruments · Codex studies · October 2026

T he first commit landed on March 31, 2026. It was called Echo Maps then: a platform for sensing rooms with WiFi, big ambitions, and not much hardware. Within a day it had a new name, a dark interface borrowed from the language of Google Home, and a plan to turn the radio waves in a room into a living picture of it.

Twelve days later the commits stopped. The work did not. For nearly six months I kept using and testing the tool in my own home, collecting data and presenting the research to the technical community, but not one new feature was committed. Then, on October 6, the code started again, this time in a Motel 6, and almost everything that had made the spring version look impressive came out.

I stopped asking what the screen could show and started asking what I could stand behind.

This is the story of that change, told as a timeline, a feature map and a set of drawings in the style of a Renaissance notebook, because the first Echo Vue was exactly the kind of thing you sketch before you know if it can be built.

Chapter I · March 31 to April 12

The vision.

Twelve days of building at full speed. The aim was a digital twin of a home: who is in each room, where they are standing, how they are moving, how they are breathing. Here is what got built, and what it was for.

  1. Mar 31

    First commit: Echo Maps

    A CSI sensing platform, a web dashboard, Google sign-in and a backend, all stood up in a single day. By night it had a new name, Echo Vue, a dark interface inspired by Google Home, a calibration wizard, a presence tab and a Cameras tab.

    Intention: Turn the WiFi in a room into a picture of the room.

  2. Before the code

    Measure first

    Before a room went into Echo Vue it was measured by hand. A Klein Tools laser gave the distance to each wall, and a voltage detector found the outlets and live wiring inside them. Those numbers became room dimensions, square footage and floor plans, for homes first and then for facilities, buildings and factories.

    Intention: Understand the physical space before trying to sense anything in it.

  3. Apr 1

    A face for it

    A light theme, a transparent logo, emoji icons and a watermark.

    Intention: Make a sensing tool feel like a home product, not a lab bench.

  4. Apr 2

    The skeleton

    Real-time skeletal tracking from a camera using TensorFlow.js MoveNet, wired into a 3D viewer. Bluetooth discovery that named the manufacturer and operating system of nearby devices, and a protocol for tethering a Bluetooth address to a person's radio signature. The first research white paper.

    Intention: Know who is in the room and follow them.

  5. Apr 3

    It follows you

    Cloud sync across devices, network fingerprints, an architecture infographic, and a stream of fixes to keep the 3D view alive.

    Intention: One home, many screens.

  6. Apr 7

    The map

    A live floor-plan map with pulsing presence dots, a presence engine, household profiles and visitor tracking, a floor-plan editor, and an expanded 3D skeleton viewer. Cross-modal detection that fused camera, radio and Bluetooth.

    Intention: See a home as a digital twin: who, where, moving how.

  7. Apr 8

    Anchors

    Editable beacon identities that remember corrections, and a WiFi router anchor for triangulation.

    Intention: Pin the radio picture to real positions in the room.

  8. Apr 12

    The last spring commit

    A mobile room scanner for visual mapping and calibration. Then the project went quiet.

    Intention: Map the room with a camera, then sense it with radio.

How it used to work

The skeleton.

What it did

A Cameras tab let you register and calibrate a camera for each room. Its video fed TensorFlow.js MoveNet, which found 17 body points; those were expanded to a 33-point skeleton and drawn in a 3D room, so you could watch a person walk, sit and wave as a wireframe. A mobile room scanner later used a phone camera to map the space itself.

What it was for

To show that a space could be understood as a body in a room, and to give every other feature, from positions to activity, something concrete to hang on.

Why it is gone

A skeleton identifies a person, and it came from a camera. A hallway only needs to know whether someone is in it. When the project moved into a public building, the camera code and the 3D viewer came out.

One honest footnote belongs on the drawing. The original design streamed raw radio data to a server, where a band-pass filter could pull out breathing and heart-rate bands. But in the browser, whenever no live stream was connected, the vitals panel filled itself with a generated sine wave around 15.5 breaths a minute and 72 beats. It looked finished. It was a placeholder for a measurement that did not exist yet.

How it used to work

The map of dots.

The live floor plan was the spring version at its most seductive. Draw a plan of your home, and each person appeared as a pulsing dot, leaving a trail as they moved from room to room. It was fed by a presence engine, household profiles and visitor tracking.

In the demonstration, much of that came from a simulated radio scan: a pool of imagined phones, tablets and watches placed in rooms. It was a convincing preview of what a sensed home could feel like. It was a preview, and the October rebuild exists because a preview is not a product.

Where it started

Measure first.

Before Echo Vue sensed a room, it had to know the room. The earliest work leaned on an ordinary tradesman's instrument, a Klein Tools laser for the distance to the walls, plus a voltage detector for finding outlets and live power inside them without opening anything up.

From those readings Echo Vue learned the square footage and shape of a space, and where power and the router sat within it. That gave the radio data something physical to be read against, and it is how a home, and then a facility, a building or a factory floor, could be understood as a place rather than a list of signals.

April 12 to October 6

One hundred and seventy-seven days in the field.

The repository went quiet; the project did not. Through those months I kept using and testing the tool at home, collecting data, and presenting the research to the technical community. No new features were committed, which is why the calendar below shows a long stretch without a mark on it.

Chapter II · October 6 to October 11

The motel.

Six days, and a different question. Not “what can this show?” but “what can be defended in a hallway full of strangers?” The answer shaped every commit that followed.

  1. Oct 6

    Back, with a clipboard

    A WiFi site survey and a hotel auto-setup that models a property: floors, hallways, guest rooms and amenities.

    Intention: Stop modelling a living room. Model a real building.

  2. Oct 7

    The camera comes out

    Public-area sensing zones, a hallway per floor, a privacy policy, and continuous CSI presence sensing. Real WiFi captured on site became the beacons. All camera code was removed.

    Intention: The question was occupancy, not identity. A hallway should not need a face.

  3. Oct 8

    A bridge you can leave behind

    Firmware that joins the property's WiFi on its own, bridges you name and assign to an area from the dashboard, a Public Areas view in place of the room list, and the 3D viewer removed.

    Intention: Make the device work in a building you do not live in.

  4. Oct 9

    Breathing, measured

    A breathing-rate estimate from the CSI spectrum, anonymised nearby-device counts, and people and pets told apart by distinct breathing rates. Each number is labelled an estimate.

    Intention: Say only what the signal can support, and say so when it cannot.

  5. Oct 10

    A second bridge

    Firmware for a 4.0-inch ESP32-32E wall display, and a fix that finally lit the Freenove screen.

    Intention: A device that looks like part of the wall.

  6. Oct 11

    The device talks back

    The live dashboard on the bridge's own screen, a History page of anonymous aggregates kept for 90 days, AI-written insights, and the M6 Case Study.

    Intention: A readout you can read without trusting anyone.

The hardware

From a webcam to a wall plate.

The spring version needed a laptop and a webcam. The October version needed something you could leave in a hallway: a small board with a radio, a processor, and a screen that tells you exactly what it is doing, down to its area, its network and its health.

What changed, and why

The spectacle went first.

The spring build was the better demo. A glowing skeleton and a map of moving dots make a room feel alive, and they photograph beautifully. But a good deal of that life was borrowed: from a camera, from a simulated scan, from a formula standing in for a signal.

Three things pulled it apart. Honesty: a screen should never show a number the system did not measure. Privacy: a camera, a skeleton and a per-person profile all identify people, and the point of the product was never who. Reality: a bridge in a hotel hallway has to work on a network you do not control, be set up from a dashboard, and say what it is doing on its own screen.

What replaced the spectacle is quieter and true to its inputs. Breathing is estimated from the radio spectrum and shown with its confidence, or not shown. People and pets are counted from distinct breathing rates and labelled as estimates. Nothing identifies anyone.

Some of the spring ideas are gone for good, heart rate and the per-person skeleton among them. Others came back in a form that can be defended. The question I carry from the motel is the one in the pull quote above: not what the screen can show, but what I can stand behind.

The reasoning behind the privacy choices, and the legal questions it left open, is in the M6 Case Study.

The next chapter is the building.

Illy Bridge 2 and Illy Bridge Pro are in prototype. Echo Vue is on its way from experiment to product.
